Heal to Lead · Episode 1: The Boardroom Ghost

Why You Are the Leader Who Gets Triggered So Easily, and How to Finally Fix It

By Banio Luiji Nobert · 6 min read

There is a man I know, and I will not tell you his real name because he did not give me permission to use it, only permission to tell his story so that other leaders might find themselves inside it the way he once found himself inside a mirror he did not ask for. Let us call him Peter. Peter runs a growing company out of Kampala, the kind of company that shows up in newspapers with words like promising and disruptive attached to its name, and Peter himself is the sort of man that younger staff members quietly admire from a respectful distance, the way one admires a mountain rather than climbs it.

But something happens to Peter in every single leadership meeting, something so small and so quick that most people in the room never notice it, and even Peter, for many years, did not notice it either. The moment a junior member of his team disagrees with him, even gently, even with the kind of respect that should make disagreement easy to receive, something tightens behind Peter's eyes. His voice drops half a register. He becomes, for the length of that meeting and sometimes for the rest of the day, a slightly different man than the one who walked into the room. Nobody in that meeting can name what has happened.

Peter could not name it either, not for a long time. He assumed, as many leaders assume, that this was simply what leadership felt like under pressure, a kind of professional weather that passed through every strong executive from time to time. It was only later, in a conversation that had nothing to do with business at all, that he began to notice where the tightening actually came from. He remembered a dinner table from thirty years earlier, a father who did not raise his voice so much as lower it when a child spoke out of turn, and a small boy who learned, long before he learned to read, that disagreement from someone smaller than you was a kind of danger that had to be managed rather than welcomed.

The Meeting Before the Meeting

Here is the thing about that boy at the dinner table. He did not stay at the dinner table. He grew up, he built a career, he became the kind of man other people call a natural leader, and somewhere in that growing up, nobody told him that the small boy would be coming to work with him every single day. Not as a memory he could visit on purpose, but as a ghost who sits quietly in every boardroom, waiting for the particular sound of being challenged by someone junior, and then reaching for the controls without asking permission first.

This is what I mean when I say that the child you once were is still running your meetings. Not in some vague, symbolic sense that makes for a nice line in a leadership book and nothing more. I mean it literally. The tightened voice, the sudden defensiveness, the flash of something that looks like anger but is closer to fear, these are rarely reactions to what is actually happening in the room. They are older reactions, borrowed from an earlier room, wearing the costume of the present moment so convincingly that even the leader having them cannot always tell the difference.

Perhaps you are reading this and already thinking of your own version of Peter's dinner table. Perhaps it was not a father's lowered voice but a teacher's public correction, or a season of being the newest, smallest, least certain person in a house that did not have room for uncertainty. It does not matter, particularly, which room it was. What matters is whether you have ever asked yourself, honestly and without the usual leadership language to hide behind, what exactly gets triggered in you and where it first learned to protect itself.

Why This Is Not Weakness

I want to say clearly, because I know how this can be misread, that none of this makes Peter a weak leader, and it would not make you one either. If anything, the opposite is closer to the truth. The leaders who never examine this are the ones whose teams learn, slowly and without ever saying so out loud, to stop bringing them the truth. Not because the leader is cruel, but because the cost of disagreement has quietly become too high, and people are remarkably good at reading a room even when the leader in it cannot yet read himself.

What changed for Peter was not a personality overhaul, and it was certainly not becoming a different man overnight. What changed was smaller and, in its own way, harder. He began to notice the tightening as it happened, in real time, and to ask himself a single question before responding: is this meeting asking something dangerous of me, or is it only asking something unfamiliar? Almost always, once he paused long enough to ask, the answer was the second one. And unfamiliar, unlike dangerous, does not need managing. It only needs staying present for.

What This Means for the Way You Lead

Maybe this is the part worth sitting with longest. Leadership is often taught as a set of skills to acquire, communication, delegation, strategy, vision, and all of these matter. But underneath every one of those skills sits a quieter, less discussed layer: the leader's own unresolved history, running in the background, shaping which meetings feel safe and which ones feel like a threat to be neutralised. You cannot delegate your way out of a triggered nervous system. You cannot strategise around a reaction that was formed before you had language for it.

What you can do, and what Peter is still learning to do, one meeting at a time, is begin to notice. Not to fix everything at once, and certainly not to arrive at some finished, healed version of yourself that never reacts to anything again. That is not the goal, and I would be suspicious of anyone who claimed to have reached it. The goal, more modestly and more honestly, is simply to know when the ghost has entered the room, so that you, and not it, are the one leading the meeting.

This opens the Heal to Lead series, drawn from the book of the same name. The next episode looks at the two kinds of wounds every leader carries, often without knowing it, and what each one quietly asks of the people around them.
